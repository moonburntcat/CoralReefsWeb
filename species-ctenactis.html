Ctenactis (Disc Coral)

Ctenactis—often called disc corals—are free-living fungiids with a flattened oval skeleton, a single long groove down the center, and radiating septa that resemble comb teeth. Their ability to inflate tissue, shed sediment, and even move slowly across sandy bottoms helps them thrive on wave-sheltered reef slopes.

Morphology

Colonies grow as single, thick discs 15–35 cm long with rounded ends and low costae that radiate like spokes. A central mouth groove with multiple mouths allows the coral to channel food along mucus tracks. Tissue can inflate several centimeters, forming a cushion that protects the fragile skeleton from abrasion.

Distribution

Ctenactis species occur across the Indo-Pacific from the Red Sea and East Africa to Micronesia and northern Australia. They favor lagoon floors and upper reef slopes between 3 and 30 meters where gentle currents deliver plankton while fine sediments settle out.

Ecological Role

As mobile sand-dwelling corals, they stabilize loose substrates and create microhabitats for small crustaceans. Nightly feeding captures zooplankton and organic detritus, recycling nutrients and supporting reef food webs. Their resilience makes them model organisms for studying sediment tolerance.
